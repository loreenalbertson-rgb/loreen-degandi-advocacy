You Deserve to Be Heard: Why Patient Advocacy Became So Important to Me

Patient advocacy became important to me through personal experience, deep research, and a growing conviction that even in the most complicated medical situations, the person should never get lost in the medicine.

By Loreen DeGandiIndependent Patient Advocate

I did not find patient advocacy because I was looking for a career. I found it because someone I love needed an advocate.

What began as trying to understand an incredibly complicated medical situation quickly became hours of research, questions, emails, phone calls, medical records, medications, specialists, rehabilitation programs, resources, and conversations with people across different parts of the healthcare system. There were so many things I did not know when I started, and rather than allowing that uncertainty to stop me, I learned.

I researched until unfamiliar medical terminology began to make sense. I learned how to organize complicated information, how to look for credible sources, how to prepare better questions, and how to keep searching when one answer only seemed to lead to five more questions. I reached out to specialists and organizations, looked into rehabilitation and neurological care, followed up on services and referrals, and tried to understand not only what was happening medically, but what else might be available and who might be able to help.

Somewhere in the middle of all of that, I realized something about myself that has stayed with me ever since: this is where my heart is.

Research is one of the ways I care. I want to understand the information because there is a real person on the other side of the question.

I genuinely love research. I love taking something that feels impossibly complicated and slowly making it understandable. I love finding the resource someone did not know existed, figuring out which question may need to be asked next, and helping organize all of the pieces when everything feels scattered. But more than anything, I care deeply about the person at the center of it all.

I Know How Overwhelming Healthcare Can Feel

When you or someone you love becomes seriously ill, there is no instruction manual that appears and tells you exactly what to do next. You may suddenly be hearing terminology you have never heard before while trying to understand medications, test results, specialists, insurance, rehabilitation, referrals, and treatment decisions. At the same time, you are still a person who may be frightened, exhausted, grieving, or trying desperately to keep everything together.

You might spend hours researching at night because you are terrified of overlooking something important. You might walk away from an appointment and realize you forgot half of the questions you wanted to ask. You may hear an explanation and realize later that you did not really understand it, or notice that something has changed in the person you love while struggling to explain why that change feels significant.

Sometimes, underneath all of that, you may simply feel like nobody is listening.

Those moments are a large part of what made me want to become the kind of person who can stand beside another patient or family and say, “We can work through this together.”

Research Should Help Us Ask Better Questions

I am naturally someone who wants to understand the why behind things. When I come across a complicated medical question, I do not want the quickest answer. I want to understand the condition, the terminology, the available research, the resources, the specialists involved, and what questions may still need to be asked.

That curiosity has become one of my greatest strengths as an advocate, but I also believe strongly in knowing the boundaries of that research. Research cannot replace a physician's clinical judgment, and no amount of reading makes every patient's situation identical. Medicine is complex, evidence changes, and good advocacy requires humility as much as persistence.

What careful research can do is help us ask better questions. It can help a family understand terminology that once felt frightening, uncover programs or resources they did not know existed, organize a complicated medical history into something easier to communicate, or help someone walk into an appointment feeling more prepared and less overwhelmed.

To me, research is not about trying to know more than someone's healthcare team. It is about helping the patient become a more informed participant in their own care.

The Person Should Never Get Lost in the Medicine

One of the strongest lessons I have learned through advocacy is how easily a human being can become a diagnosis. A complicated medical situation can quickly turn into lists of medications, test results, procedures, appointments, referrals, and clinical terminology. Those things matter, but somewhere underneath all of them is still a person.

Someone has favorite music and people they love. Someone is frightened. Someone wants answers. Someone may be unable to speak for themselves. Someone may simply need another person to notice that they are uncomfortable, confused, hurting, or struggling.

I believe the patient's humanity should remain at the center of every conversation about their care.

I believe patients deserve to be spoken to with dignity even when they cannot respond. I believe families deserve explanations they can understand. I believe quality of life matters, subtle concerns deserve to be heard, and a person's income, education, disability, diagnosis, ability to communicate, or medical complexity should never determine whether they deserve compassion.

Advocacy Does Not Have to Mean Conflict

Being an advocate does not mean assuming healthcare professionals are wrong. Some of the people I have encountered in healthcare have been incredibly compassionate, thoughtful, and willing to listen, and I believe good advocacy can strengthen that relationship rather than work against it.

Sometimes advocacy means preparing a clear list of questions before an appointment. Sometimes it means helping a family put months of medical information into a timeline so a new specialist can understand what has happened. Sometimes it means researching resources or programs, asking for clarification because something does not make sense, following up because an important referral appears to have stalled, or documenting changes so the healthcare team has better information.

And sometimes it simply means saying, “Can we talk about this again? I am still concerned.”

There is nothing disrespectful about asking thoughtful questions about your healthcare. You are not difficult because you want to understand. You are allowed to ask why something is being recommended, take notes, request clarification, bring someone with you for support, and seek another qualified opinion when appropriate.

The Details Matter to Me

I am a detail person. If a medication changes and someone suddenly seems different, I believe that observation deserves to be documented. If a family repeatedly notices the same response, they should feel comfortable bringing it to the medical team. If there is a program, specialist, benefit, rehabilitation option, or community resource that could potentially help, I want to know about it.

If someone's medical story spans several hospitals or providers, I want to help put those pieces together so the bigger picture becomes easier to see. Sometimes one seemingly small detail changes the next question, and sometimes the right question changes the direction of an entire conversation.

That is why compassion and careful research belong together in my approach. One without the other is not enough for me. I want to understand the information, but I never want to forget why I am looking for it.

I Want to Support the Whole Person

My heart for advocacy is not limited to one diagnosis or one area of medicine. I care about the patient who has been searching for answers for years, the parent trying to navigate healthcare for their child, the person living with a disability who is tired of having concerns dismissed, and the family suddenly facing a devastating medical or neurological crisis.

I care about the caregiver who has been so focused on everyone else that nobody has asked how they are doing. I care about the person with a new diagnosis who goes home, opens their phone, and searches the name of their condition because they have no idea where else to begin. I care about the patient who is nervous to ask another question because they are afraid of being seen as difficult, and I especially care about the person who cannot advocate for themselves at all.

Those patients need people around them who continue to see them as human beings with preferences, relationships, comfort, dignity, and value.

What I Hope to Offer

I am building my work in patient advocacy around the things I have discovered I do best: listening carefully, researching thoroughly, organizing complicated information, finding resources, preparing thoughtful questions, communicating clearly, and following through.

I cannot diagnose a medical condition, prescribe treatment, or replace a licensed healthcare professional, and I would never want to. What I can do is help someone feel more prepared for the conversations that matter. I can help research resources and services, organize medical information, prepare questions for an appointment, build a timeline when a medical history feels overwhelming, identify patient rights and support resources, and help families keep track of information when there are simply too many moving pieces.

And I can listen. That last part may sound simple, but I do not think it is. Sometimes being truly listened to is the beginning of everything else.

This Is More Than Work to Me

There are careers people choose because they are good at them, and then there are things that feel connected to who you are. Patient advocacy has become that for me. The more I learn, the more certain I become that I want to spend my time helping people navigate the moments when healthcare feels confusing, frightening, frustrating, or impossibly complicated.

I want to continue learning. I want to continue researching. I want to become an even stronger advocate, and I want every patient or family who comes across my work to feel something very simple:

You matter here.

You do not need to explain your entire medical history perfectly before I will listen. You do not need to know the right terminology. You do not need to already know which specialist you need or what question you should ask next.

You can start with, “I don't know what to do.”

We can start there.

Because behind every diagnosis is a person, and behind every complicated medical situation is someone hoping that another human being will care enough to listen, look closer, ask thoughtful questions, and help them figure out what comes next.

That is the kind of advocate I want to be: compassionate enough to listen, curious enough to keep learning, thorough enough to research the details, persistent enough to keep looking for resources and answers, and humble enough to recognize when a question belongs with someone who has greater clinical expertise.

Most of all, I want patients and families to know that when they feel overwhelmed or unheard, they do not have to carry every part of it alone.

This is where my heart is. And I am here to help.
