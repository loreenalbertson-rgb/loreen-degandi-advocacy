When Your Loved One Can't Speak: Being Their Voice in Healthcare

When words are not possible, dignity, comfort, preferences, and connection still matter. Here are practical ways to help someone you love be heard.

By Loreen DeGandiIndependent Patient Advocate

There is a particular kind of helplessness in standing beside someone you love and realizing they cannot simply tell you what hurts, what they need, or what they want.

Maybe they have experienced a brain injury, a stroke, a serious illness, or a condition that affects speech. Maybe they use a ventilator or are too weak to communicate as they once did. The silence can be frightening. But an inability to speak does not automatically mean an inability to understand, feel, have preferences, or communicate in other ways.

Not being able to speak should never mean being left out of your own care.

Begin With the Person, Not the Diagnosis

Talk to your loved one respectfully. Explain who is in the room and what is happening before care begins. Use their name, offer choices when possible, and give them time to respond. Ask staff to speak to the patient rather than only about them.

Do not assume that a person understands everything—or nothing—based on appearance alone. Ask the clinical team what is known about hearing, awareness, language comprehension, vision, and communication abilities.

Look for Ways They May Communicate

Communication may involve facial expressions, eye movements, gestures, writing, pictures, assistive technology, or reliable yes/no responses. A speech-language pathologist may be able to assess communication and suggest appropriate tools.

Families can share observations without interpreting every movement as a definite message. Note what happened, when it happened, and whether it repeats. A blink, squeeze, or look is not automatically a reliable answer; the care team can help test consistency and rule out reflexive or involuntary movements.

  • Ask whether a communication assessment is appropriate.
  • Find out whether hearing, vision, fatigue, pain, or medication effects could interfere with responses.
  • Offer simple choices only if the team has established a dependable way for the patient to answer.
  • Allow extra processing time and avoid overwhelming them with several questions at once.

Become a Careful Observer

Families often know someone's usual habits, expressions, and reactions. That familiarity can help clinicians notice changes, but it should complement—not replace—medical assessment.

Keep a short log of meaningful observations: date, time, what you saw, how long it lasted, what was happening around the patient, and who you informed. Record observations as facts rather than conclusions. For example, “turned eyes toward my voice twice” is more useful than “definitely understood everything.”

Report new or concerning changes promptly to the care team. If breathing, consciousness, or another condition suddenly becomes an emergency, seek immediate medical help.

Ask Questions That Protect Comfort and Safety

When a person cannot describe symptoms, ask how the team checks for pain, discomfort, distress, and unmet needs. Clinicians may use validated observation-based tools appropriate to the patient's condition.

  • How are pain and discomfort assessed when they cannot self-report?
  • What signs should we watch for, and whom should we tell?
  • How are positioning, skin protection, mouth care, and sleep addressed?
  • When will communication ability be reassessed?
  • Who coordinates updates across nursing, therapy, and medical teams?

It is reasonable to ask for the care plan in plain language and to request clarification when an answer does not make sense.

Understand Your Role in Decisions

Being a loving family member and being legally authorized to make healthcare decisions are not always the same thing. A patient may still be able to make some or all decisions with appropriate communication support. Decision-making capacity is specific to the decision and circumstances, and difficulty speaking alone does not establish incapacity.

Ask the team how they assess the patient's decision-making ability, whether an advance directive or healthcare representative is documented, and which state laws or facility procedures apply. When someone else is authorized to decide, that person's role is to follow the patient's known wishes and applicable decision-making standards—not simply their own preferences. For legal questions, seek qualified local advice.

Speak Up Without Turning Every Conversation Into a Fight

Good advocacy can be both respectful and persistent. Start with a clear observation, explain your concern, and ask for a specific next step.

“I've noticed a change from what I usually see. Could we document it and discuss whether an assessment is needed?”
“Can you explain how you're checking for pain when they can't tell us directly?”
“Who can help us explore communication tools that might work for them?”

If a concern remains unresolved, ask for the charge nurse, attending clinician, patient relations department, or a care conference, depending on the situation. Document names, dates, answers, and follow-up plans.

Protect Their Dignity in Everyday Moments

Advocacy is not limited to tests, treatments, and meetings. It is also asking that the curtains be closed during personal care, that someone explain a procedure before touching the patient, and that their preferences, relationships, and identity remain visible.

Bring familiar music, photos, or comforting items if permitted and appropriate. Ask about their preferred routines. Remember that the patient is still a person with a history and a life beyond the medical chart.

Take Care of the Person Doing the Advocating

When you are worried that something might be missed, stepping away can feel impossible. But exhaustion makes organizing information and communicating harder. Share tasks with trusted people when appropriate, ask for a scheduled update rather than repeatedly chasing information, and give yourself permission to rest.

You do not need to notice every movement, know every medical term, or have an answer to every uncertainty to be a meaningful advocate.

One Small Step You Can Take Today

Write down three things: one observation you want the team to know, one question about comfort or communication, and one person responsible for following up. That is a real beginning.

Your loved one's voice is more than spoken words. Advocacy means making room for their needs, their dignity, and every appropriate opportunity for them to participate.

You don't have to navigate this alone.

Caregiver Checklist

Questions worth asking

  • How is comfort assessed?
  • Can a speech-language pathologist help?
  • Which responses are reliable?
  • Who coordinates the care plan?
  • How can the patient participate in decisions?
  • Who should receive new observations?
Remember

Communication may look different, but dignity is never optional.
