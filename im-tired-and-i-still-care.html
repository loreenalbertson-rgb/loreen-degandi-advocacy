I’m Tired, and I Still Care

A heartfelt reflection on loving someone through complicated care, carrying uncertainty, and needing support while you advocate.

By Loreen DeGandiIndependent Patient Advocate

I wish love made this easier.

I wish loving someone enough could make the answers come faster. Make the right person call back. Make the next step clear. I wish there were a way to explain how much someone means to you and have that be enough to make everything okay.

But love does not give us that kind of control. That is one of the hardest parts.

My path into advocacy began with someone I love. Before there was a website or a name for the work, there was the need to understand. To ask another question. To find out whether there was something else we should be considering. There was a person who mattered to me, and so much I did not know how to carry.

That is still at the heart of this for me.

The part that does not fit in an update

When someone asks how things are going, it can be easier to explain the next appointment or the next phone call than to explain what the waiting feels like.

How do you put all of it into an answer? The hope. The fear of hoping too much. The frustration of needing help and having to figure out who to ask for it. The way ordinary life keeps asking things of you while part of your heart is somewhere else.

You still have groceries to buy. Messages to answer. People who need you. Meanwhile, there is a question sitting in the back of your mind that you cannot put down.

And sometimes you are tired of being told you are strong. Sometimes you would rather someone ask what they can take off your plate.

You can be grateful that someone is still here and heartbroken about how much has changed.

There is room for both. You do not owe anyone a cheerful version of something that hurts.

I want us to be able to tell the truth

I care deeply about advocacy. I believe in asking questions, looking for answers, and making sure people are heard. I also want to be honest about the cost of caring this much when the situation is uncertain.

It can be exhausting.

That sentence deserves to stand on its own. It does not need to be followed immediately by a lesson or something positive.

There are families trying to understand information they never wanted to learn. People afraid to miss a call. People wondering whether they asked enough, noticed enough, or explained themselves clearly enough. People who need someone to say, “I understand why this matters to you. Let’s go through it again.”

Being upset does not make your question less worthy of an answer. Needing an explanation twice does not mean you are incapable. Crying in a conversation does not erase everything you came prepared to say.

I want healthcare to have room for people as they actually are when they are frightened.

The person I keep coming back to

There is so much language around complicated care. So many details to organize. It is easy for a conversation to become entirely about what needs to happen next.

I keep coming back to the person at the center of it.

Someone with relationships. A history. Things they enjoy. Things they would choose for themselves. Someone whose comfort matters now, even when the future is unclear.

I want us to remember to talk to them. To explain what we are doing. To make room for their wishes and whatever ways they can participate. To give them the ordinary kindness we would want for ourselves.

Love can make us desperate for improvement. I understand that longing. I also believe a person's worth is already whole. They do not have to reach a milestone to deserve tenderness, attention, and dignity.

Hope is hard to hold sometimes

I wish I could offer every family a promise about how their story will turn out. I cannot.

I do not want to give people certainty I do not have. I want to help make room for honest answers, thoughtful questions, and care that sees the whole person.

Some days, hope may be about what could become possible. Other days, it may be about comfort, a little connection, or finally having a conversation where you feel heard.

You do not have to feel hopeful every minute to love someone well. Fear does not cancel out love. Neither does exhaustion.

And the outcome of someone's illness or injury is not a measure of how hard their family fought. Families already carry enough without being made to believe they could have controlled everything by doing more.

There has to be room for you, too

If you are the person keeping track of the details, I want to say this gently: you are a person in this story, too.

Your needs still count. You are allowed to need a meal, sleep, company, or a conversation about something ordinary. You are allowed to have a good moment in the middle of a painful season.

You can ask someone to handle a phone call. Sit beside you. Write something down while you listen. You can tell someone, “I do not need you to fix this. I just need you to stay with me for a little while.”

Stepping away to rest does not erase the care you have given. You should not have to wear yourself down to prove how much someone matters.

Why I am still here

This is why advocacy matters to me so personally. I want the person asking the questions to have someone beside them, too.

Someone willing to listen long enough to understand the concern. Someone who can help sort through the information when everything feels tangled. Someone who remembers that a request for an update may be coming from a person who has been worried all day.

I cannot make every answer easier. But I care about how people are treated while they are waiting for one.

If you are reading this with a full heart and very little left in you, you do not need to find the perfect words here. You do not need to turn your pain into something inspiring.

You can simply tell the truth.

I’m tired, and I still care.

Both can be true. You deserve care, too.

With love,
Loreen
