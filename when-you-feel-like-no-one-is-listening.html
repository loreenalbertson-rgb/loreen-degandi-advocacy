When You Feel Like No One Is Listening: How to Advocate During a Medical Appointment

You do not have to be confrontational to advocate for yourself. Sometimes advocacy is preparing your questions, describing what has changed, asking for clarification, and making sure an important concern does not disappear.

By Loreen DeGandiIndependent Patient Advocate

There is a particular kind of helplessness that comes from sitting in a medical appointment, trying to explain that something is wrong, and feeling like your words are not quite reaching the person across from you.

Maybe your symptoms are being minimized. Maybe the appointment feels rushed. Maybe you came in with ten questions and suddenly cannot remember a single one. Maybe you are advocating for someone you love and you know, deep in your gut, that something has changed, but you are struggling to make the care team understand why you are concerned.

If you have ever walked out of an appointment thinking, “I wish I had said more,” you are not alone.

Advocating for yourself does not mean arguing with your medical team. It does not mean assuming that you know more than your doctor or demanding a particular test, diagnosis, or treatment.

Good advocacy is about communication.

It is about helping your care team understand what you are experiencing, making sure you understand the plan, asking questions when something does not make sense, and making sure important concerns do not disappear simply because an appointment is short.

You deserve to be an active participant in your care.

Before the Appointment, Get the Most Important Things Out of Your Head and Onto Paper

Medical appointments can be overwhelming, especially when you are anxious, sick, exhausted, grieving, or worried about someone you love.

You do not have to rely on your memory.

Before the appointment, write down the two or three things you most need the provider to understand.

Instead of simply writing, “I feel worse,” try to include details that help tell the story. When did the problem begin? Has it changed? How often is it happening? What makes it better or worse? Is it interfering with eating, sleeping, walking, working, school, parenting, or other everyday activities? Have you noticed a new symptom or a change from the person's usual baseline?

It can also help to bring an updated list of medications, supplements, allergies, and major medical history. If your healthcare situation is complicated, a short written summary may make it easier to communicate what matters without trying to reconstruct months or years of information in the exam room.

Start With What Matters Most

Appointments can move quickly. If there is one thing you desperately need addressed, do not save it until the final two minutes.

You can begin with something as simple as:

“There are a few things I want to discuss today, but this is the concern that is most important to me.”

If you are advocating for someone else, you might say:

“I spend a lot of time with them, and I have noticed a significant change from their usual baseline. I would really like to explain what I am seeing.”

You do not need medical terminology to make an important observation. Families and caregivers often know what is normal for the person they love. Describe what you are seeing as clearly and specifically as you can.

Ask Questions Until You Understand

Sometimes people leave medical appointments with instructions they technically heard but do not truly understand.

There is no prize for pretending something makes sense when it does not.

Ask.

If a provider uses unfamiliar terminology, you can say:

“Could you explain what that means in everyday language?”

If you are given a new diagnosis, ask what it means for your everyday life. If a medication is being prescribed, ask what it is intended to help with, what you should expect from it, and what side effects you should watch for. If the plan is to wait, ask what changes should prompt you to contact the office sooner or seek more urgent care.

The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality encourages patients and families to prepare questions before medical visits and to make sure they understand the answers. Its QuestionBuilder resources were created specifically to help patients and caregivers prepare for medical appointments.

Questions are not disrespectful. They are part of informed, engaged healthcare.

When You Feel Dismissed, Bring the Conversation Back to the Concern

Sometimes the hardest situation is not confusion. It is feeling unheard.

You explain the problem and receive a quick reassurance that does not address what is worrying you. You can remain respectful without abandoning your concern.

Try bringing the conversation back to specifics:

“I understand that this may not look concerning based on what you are seeing today. What worries me is that this is very different from their normal behavior.”

Or:

“I hear what you are saying. I am still concerned because these symptoms are getting worse rather than better. What possibilities are we considering?”

Or simply:

“I do not feel like my main concern has been answered yet. Could we go back to it for a moment?”

You do not need to become confrontational to be persistent. Calmly repeating the question is still advocacy.

Ask About the Reasoning Behind the Plan

When people feel ignored, it can be tempting to focus on getting a particular test, referral, or treatment. Sometimes a more productive question is:

“Can you help me understand how you reached that decision?”

If a test is not recommended, ask what makes the provider feel it is unnecessary right now. If a referral is not being made, ask what circumstances would make one appropriate. If symptoms are believed to have a particular explanation, ask whether there are other possibilities and what signs would cause the team to reconsider.

You may ultimately agree with the plan. You may decide you want another qualified opinion. Either way, understanding the reasoning gives you much more information than leaving with a simple yes or no.

Bring Someone With You When You Need Support

You do not always have to do this alone.

A trusted family member, friend, caregiver, or advocate can listen, take notes, remember questions, and help you communicate when emotions make it difficult to find the words.

Before the appointment, decide what role you want that person to have. Maybe you only want emotional support. Maybe you want them to take notes. Maybe you want them to speak up if an important question gets forgotten.

There is something powerful about knowing another person is sitting beside you and listening too.

Take Notes

Medical appointments contain a surprising amount of information. Write down diagnoses, medication changes, tests that were ordered, referrals that were discussed, follow up instructions, and anything you are supposed to monitor at home.

If you are too overwhelmed to take notes yourself, ask the person with you to do it.

Before you leave, make sure you can answer three basic questions:

What do we think is happening?

What are we doing next?

What should make me contact someone or seek help sooner?

If you cannot answer those questions, ask for clarification.

If You Still Do Not Feel Heard

Sometimes you can prepare carefully, communicate respectfully, ask thoughtful questions, and still leave feeling that something important was missed.

That does not mean you have to give up.

Depending on the situation, your next step might be sending a follow up message through the patient portal, scheduling another appointment, asking whether another clinician in the practice can evaluate the concern, requesting an appropriate specialist referral, or seeking a second opinion.

If your concern involves a hospital or health system, there may also be a patient relations, patient experience, ombudsman, social work, or advocacy department that can help you understand available pathways for raising concerns.

Keep documentation of important conversations, dates, symptoms, medication changes, test results, and the questions you have asked. Not because every medical relationship should become adversarial, but because complicated care becomes much easier to understand when there is a clear timeline.

Advocacy Does Not Have to Be Loud

One of the biggest misconceptions about patient advocacy is that you have to be fearless, forceful, or confrontational.

You do not.

You can have anxiety and still advocate. You can cry during the appointment and still advocate. You can need notes because your mind goes blank and still advocate. You can say, “I do not understand,” “I am still worried,” or “Can we talk about this again?” and still be a strong advocate.

Sometimes advocacy is simply refusing to let an important concern disappear.

Your voice belongs in the room. Your experience matters. Your questions matter.

And when you are speaking for someone you love who cannot fully speak for themselves, your careful observations and persistence can become an incredibly important part of their story.

You do not have to know every medical term. You do not have to have every answer. You only have to keep asking the questions that matter.

And if doing that alone feels overwhelming, you are allowed to ask for help.
