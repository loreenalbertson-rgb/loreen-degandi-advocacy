When Someone You Love Cannot Advocate for Themselves

When someone cannot tell us what they need, advocacy can become one of the deepest ways we care for them. This is about noticing, documenting, asking questions, protecting dignity, and making sure the person never disappears inside the medicine.

By Loreen DeGandiIndependent Patient Advocate

There is a different kind of fear that comes with loving someone who cannot tell you what they need.

Maybe they cannot speak because of a brain injury. Maybe they are critically ill, sedated, very young, cognitively impaired, living with a communication disability, or facing a medical condition that has changed the way they interact with the world. Whatever the reason, suddenly the people who love them may find themselves carrying a responsibility they never expected to have.

You begin watching more closely. You notice the small changes. You remember which medication was started before they became sleepier. You notice that they seem uncomfortable in a certain position. You recognize a facial expression that other people may not understand. You remember what they were able to do yesterday that they are not doing today.

You start keeping track of appointments, conversations, medications, specialists, symptoms, therapies, referrals, and questions because the person you love cannot do those things for themselves. Somewhere along the way, you realize that you have become their voice.

That is an enormous responsibility. It can also be one of the deepest expressions of love.

Advocacy Begins With Seeing the Person

When someone becomes seriously ill or disabled, it can become frighteningly easy for the person themselves to disappear underneath the medical situation. Their days may become filled with medications, procedures, therapy schedules, vital signs, test results, appointments, and clinical terminology.

Those things matter, but they are not the whole person. The person in that hospital bed still has a history. They have people they love. They have preferences, routines, memories, favorite music, things that make them laugh, things that frighten them, and ways of communicating that may not always be obvious to someone meeting them for the first time.

A medical chart can tell us many important things. It cannot tell us everything about the human being it belongs to.

That is one of the reasons family involvement can matter so much. Families often know the person in ways that cannot be learned from a chart. They may recognize when something looks different, know what normally brings comfort, notice a response to a familiar voice, song, person, or touch, or recognize signs of pain or distress that are subtle to someone who does not know the patient well.

Family observations do not replace medical assessment, and not every observation will have medical significance. But they still deserve to be heard.

You Do Not Have to Be a Medical Professional to Notice a Change

One of the things I hope families understand is that you are allowed to notice things. You do not need a medical degree to say, “They seem different today.” You do not need to know exactly why something is happening before bringing it to someone's attention, and you do not need to diagnose the problem in order to describe what you are seeing.

You might notice that your loved one seems much sleepier since a medication change, that they are no longer moving an arm the way they were several days ago, that their facial expression changes when they are repositioned, or that they respond differently to one familiar voice than another.

Those observations can become useful information when they are communicated clearly and documented over time.

Sometimes advocacy begins with nothing more complicated than saying, “I have noticed something different, and I would like someone to take a closer look.”

The Small Details Can Matter

Complex medical situations often involve an incredible amount of information: medication changes, new symptoms, specialist recommendations, therapy notes, hospital transfers, lab results, appointments, referrals, and changes in alertness, movement, communication, appetite, sleep, breathing, behavior, pain, or comfort.

When everything is happening at once, individual details can easily become difficult to track. This is one of the reasons I believe documentation can be such an important part of advocacy.

You do not need to create a perfect medical record of your own. Sometimes a simple notebook or digital document is enough. Writing down dates, changes, questions, names, medications, and conversations can help you remember what happened and communicate more clearly later. It can also help you recognize patterns that may be difficult to see when you are living through each day individually.

To me, careful documentation is not about trying to prove someone wrong. It is about creating clarity. When the person you love cannot explain their own medical history or tell the next provider what has changed, organized information can help carry their story forward.

Ask the Questions They Cannot Ask

When someone cannot advocate for themselves, I often think about the questions they might ask if they were able to participate fully in the conversation.

Am I comfortable? Could something be causing me pain? Why was this medication changed? What is the goal of this treatment? What are the possible side effects? Is there another specialist who should be involved? What therapy or rehabilitation might help me? What are we watching for? What happens next? Are there other options? Has anything changed since the last evaluation? Could something be affecting my ability to respond or communicate?

These are not unreasonable questions. They are the kinds of questions that help families understand what is happening and help keep the patient at the center of the conversation.

You may not receive a simple answer every time. Medicine is complicated, and sometimes the answer truly is that nobody knows yet. But there is value in asking, in understanding why something is being done, and in making sure decisions about a person do not happen around them without anyone continually asking what is best for them.

Dignity Matters Even When We Do Not Know What Someone Understands

One of my strongest beliefs in advocacy is that uncertainty should never be a reason to stop treating someone like a person.

If someone cannot speak, speak to them anyway. If you do not know exactly how much they understand, explain what is happening anyway. Tell them before you touch them. Tell them who has entered the room. Play the music they love. Use their name. Include them in conversations about their own care whenever possible. Protect their privacy. Pay attention to their comfort. Talk about them respectfully when they are present.

There are situations in which nobody can know exactly what another person is experiencing internally, but we do not need certainty about someone's awareness before deciding that they deserve kindness.

Dignity should not depend on someone's ability to prove that they understand us.

Family Advocacy and Medical Expertise Can Work Together

Advocacy is sometimes misunderstood as fighting with doctors or assuming that healthcare professionals do not care. That is not what advocacy means to me.

Healthcare professionals bring education, training, clinical experience, and knowledge that families depend on. Families bring something different: knowledge of the person. Good care should have room for both.

A family member can say, “This is what I am seeing,” while a clinician explains what it may or may not mean medically. A caregiver can ask why a treatment is being recommended while still respecting the expertise of the person recommending it. A family can request clarification, additional evaluation, or another qualified opinion without turning the relationship into a battle.

Collaboration is powerful. At the same time, families should not feel that being respectful means they must remain silent when something concerns them.

You are allowed to ask again. You are allowed to say that an explanation did not make sense to you. You are allowed to bring notes. You are allowed to ask who is responsible for following up on a referral. You are allowed to ask whether a specialist should be involved. You are allowed to say, “I understand what you are telling me, but I am still concerned.”

Respect and persistence can exist together.

Sometimes You Will Question Yourself

One of the hardest parts of advocating for someone you love is wondering whether you are doing enough. You may wonder whether you are asking too many questions, worry that you are being difficult, replay conversations in your head afterward, or think of the question you wish you had asked.

You may spend hours researching and still feel as though you understand only a fraction of what is happening. You may feel enormous pressure because the person you love cannot simply tell everyone what they want.

That is a heavy thing to carry.

No family member can know everything. No advocate will ask every perfect question. No one can control every outcome. Sometimes the best thing you can do is keep showing up, keep learning, keep documenting, keep asking thoughtful questions, and keep making sure the person you love is not forgotten in the complexity of their care.

That matters more than you may realize.

Advocacy Is Also About Quality of Life

When someone has a serious illness or disability, conversations can become focused almost entirely on survival, diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. Those things are obviously important, but there are other questions worth asking too.

Are they comfortable? Can they spend time somewhere other than a bed? Can they go outside? Can they hear familiar music? Can they participate in therapy? Can they be positioned in a way that is more comfortable? Is there a way for them to communicate, even if it is very simple? Are there activities or sensory experiences they seem to enjoy? Can their family be involved in meaningful ways? What parts of their personality, routine, or relationships can still be included in their life?

Quality of life does not look the same for every person. That is exactly why the individual person needs to remain at the center of the discussion.

When the Patient Cannot Speak, Someone Still Needs to Listen

There are many people who may need another person to help carry their voice: a child who does not understand how to explain what hurts, a person with a developmental disability whose communication looks different from what others expect, someone with dementia who is struggling to express discomfort, a critically ill patient who is sedated, a person with a neurological injury who cannot reliably move or speak, or someone with a chronic illness who has become too exhausted to keep explaining their story over and over again.

Their circumstances may be very different, but the principle is the same.

The inability to advocate for yourself should never make your needs less important.

In fact, it is often the moment when advocacy matters most.

This Is One of the Reasons My Heart Is in This Work

I know what it feels like to care deeply about someone who cannot simply sit up and explain what they need. I know what it is like to start researching because you have questions and then realize how many more questions exist. I know what it feels like to notice small changes and wonder whether anyone else sees them, and I know how much time can go into looking for specialists, resources, rehabilitation possibilities, information, and another path forward.

Through that experience, I learned something that has changed me: I love this work.

Not because medical situations are easy. They are not. Not because advocacy can guarantee an outcome. It cannot. I love it because there is something incredibly meaningful about helping make sure a human being is still seen when their own voice has become difficult or impossible to hear.

Research became one of the ways I learned to care. Documentation became a way to protect important details. Questions became a way to keep conversations moving.

Persistence became a way of saying, “This person still matters.”

That is something I want to carry into every patient and family I support.

If This Is Where You Are Right Now

If you are currently advocating for someone who cannot advocate for themselves, I want you to know that you do not have to have every answer before you begin.

Start with what you know. Write down what you are noticing. Keep track of changes. Ask questions when something does not make sense. Ask for explanations in language you understand. Learn who is responsible for the next step. Keep the person you love at the center of every conversation, and when the situation becomes too complicated to hold in your head, organize it one piece at a time.

You can begin with one question, one concern, one observation, one next step.

If you feel overwhelmed trying to figure out where to begin, this is exactly the kind of situation where I want to help. I can help you organize information, research resources, prepare questions, build a timeline, and think through what you may want to discuss with your loved one's healthcare team.

I cannot promise an outcome, and I cannot replace medical professionals. But I can listen. I can research. I can help you organize the details. And I can stand beside you while you work to make sure the person you love remains at the center of their own care.

Because even when someone cannot speak for themselves, their voice still matters. Sometimes it simply needs someone else to help carry it.

You do not have to carry every part of this alone.
